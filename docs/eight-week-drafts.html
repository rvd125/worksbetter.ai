Eight useful discovery drafts

Prepared 1 October 2026. Unpublished drafts for Works Better. No client records or measured savings. Proposed weekly order, not a new publishing schedule.

1. Which number is right? A reporting handover checklist

Before choosing a new dashboard, ask two people to explain one number that matters. If they use different definitions or reporting periods, connecting the tools is only part of the job.

Start with one disputed measure

It might be completed work, open enquiries, available capacity or invoiced revenue. Write down its definition, source, reporting period and owner. Include what the number excludes. Then record when each source was refreshed and which references should agree.

A fictional example: one team counts completed jobs; another counts invoices issued. Both call the number “finished work”. Neither count is necessarily wrong. They answer different questions.

Keep the unresolved part visible

Name the person who can explain a difference and the decision the team can make while it remains open. Missing data should not quietly become zero, and a fresh extract should not be treated as a reconciliation.

2. A new starter is due. Who owns each document handover?

A new starter needs several people to complete different tasks. A document being uploaded does not tell the next person whether it is complete, checked or ready to use.

Choose one document

Use a fictional starter and a fictional document. Record the event that starts the handover, what information the receiving role needs, who checks it and who owns the next task. Keep personal employee information out of this exercise.

  1. What makes this document ready for review?
  2. Who confirms that it is complete?
  3. What task can start after that confirmation?
  4. Who deals with a missing or incorrect item?

Follow the incomplete path

Imagine the file arrived but one required item is missing. Write down which task should wait, who gets the exception and what counts as a completed correction. A reminder should point to a clear task and owner.

Explore the fictional employee-onboarding demonstration. It is an illustrative example, not a claim about a client implementation. Then describe one document handover to Renzo: the trigger, tools and outcome you want. The first conversation establishes fit; any paid review or build has an agreed scope and fee.

3. Give each step a job: record, rule, draft or decision

Take one short process and put a label beside each step. This gives you a practical way to discuss AI and automation without needing to choose a product first.

Use four labels

Record: the place the official information belongs. Rule: repeatable work with an agreed instruction. Draft: interpretation or preparation that may need checking. Decision: a person takes responsibility for the consequence.

In Renzo’s confirmed enquiry logic, the message arrives in Outlook. The customer database holds known customer information. AI checks for a match and, when needed, researches the company from its email domain before preparing the brief and meeting reply. Staff approval comes before Zapier sends the reply. The customer confirming a time is a separate event; connected confirmation, calendar/invitation, assignment and CRM updates follow it. Demonstration records are fictional.

Look for the unclear label

If a step does not fit, ask what it needs to know and who owns the result. A process with an unclear decision should not become less visible when software is added.

4. Two tools disagree about a customer. Which record wins?

A customer changes a detail in one place. Another tool still holds the old value. Before connecting them, agree how the business will decide which record to trust.

Choose a field and a direction

Start with one practical field: contact email, account reference or service address. Name the system that owns that field and the person responsible for a correction. Decide whether a connection should copy the value, propose a change or flag a difference.

Do not assume the newest timestamp is always right. A recent import may contain an old value. A person may have deliberately corrected the destination. Those cases need an explicit rule or review.

Try one conflict

Use a fictional customer in your worksheet. Write the two values, where they came from and the event that should resolve the conflict. Also write what happens while the difference remains unresolved.

This is a focused ownership exercise. Renzo's consulting site provides the broader systems-connection guide. For your specific conflict, describe the field, tools and review owner. Do not submit customer records or credentials. Renzo agrees scope and fees before paid work.

5. What should a person see before approving an exception?

A useful approval asks a clear question. It should show enough context for a person to decide, without making them reconstruct the whole process from several systems.

Build a review brief

Include the source record, what differs from the expected result, why that difference matters and what the proposed action would change. Name the approving role and record its decision. Also provide a decline route.

In Renzo's confirmed supplier-invoice logic, AI first checks MYOB for an existing invoice. A duplicate stops. A new invoice is compared with its purchase order. A mismatch requires an explanation before human approval. Approval then lets n8n connect filing, a MYOB record with approver details and a Gmail receipt acknowledgement. No payment is implied.

Ask about a refusal

Can the reviewer decline without the process filing or sending anything? Can a reason be retained? Does the next owner know the decision? These are useful design questions before a build.

6. An acknowledgement is not an owned next action

A customer can receive a quick receipt while their request still waits. To understand the gap, check the next owned action rather than counting automatic replies.

Follow one fictional enquiry

The message arrives. Someone or something identifies its destination. A named person receives enough context to act. If the request is unclear or that person is unavailable, an exception has its own owner and next action.

Write down when the request arrived, when an owner accepted it and what happened next. Keep those moments separate from any acknowledgement timestamp. Use the distinction to decide what the team needs to improve; do not invent a universal response target.

Check the uncertain route

Try a lead outside the usual territory, an existing customer or a team with no capacity. A routing rule should explain the outcome, and a person should be able to review an unusual handover.

The lead-routing demonstration uses fictional records. If your team keeps asking who owns a request, describe the intake, tools and follow-up you want. Renzo replies personally and agrees fees and scope before a paid engagement.

7. When a running automation should pause

A process that usually works still needs a clear response to a missing record, changed permission or failed connection. The useful question is what should stop, who should know and how work can resume.

Name the stop condition

For one running process, identify the event that would make its next action unsafe or unreliable. Examples include a duplicate invoice, an unmatched reference or a required approval that has not happened. Those are conditions to handle, not evidence that your process has already failed.

Name the owner and recovery

Who sees the exception? What should they check? Which work can continue? After correction, should the process retry the same action or begin again? Keep a record that helps avoid creating the same transaction twice.

Use fictional records to test both the normal path and the pause. A success-only demonstration cannot explain recovery. The safeguards explorer shows duplicates stopping and unresolved reporting being reviewed and rerun.

For an existing process, tell Renzo its trigger, tools and failure you need to handle. A paid review or improvement is scoped before work begins.

8. Bring one handover to a consulting conversation

You do not need technical language to explain useful work. Bring one task that loses information, takes repeated entry or needs too much chasing.

A one-page starting brief

  1. What starts the task?
  2. Which tools and roles touch it?
  3. Where does it wait, repeat or become unclear?
  4. What should a useful result look like?
  5. Which decision should stay with a person?
  6. What happens when the information is missing or unusual?

Use a fictional example to explain the shape of the process. Do not send passwords, customer records, employee details or private documents through a discovery tool.
